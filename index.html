Chapter 01

Bones

Bone is living tissue that rebuilds itself for life — but it needs raw material delivered on schedule. These four nutrients work as a single supply chain: miss one link and the others can't finish the job.

Calcium
The structural mineral

About 99% of the body's calcium is stored in bones and teeth, giving them their rigidity. Blood calcium is kept tightly stable — if diet falls short, the body withdraws it from bone to keep the rest of the body running.

Milk & yogurt Kale & bok choy Sesame & til Sardines
Vitamin D3 & D2
The absorption key

Without vitamin D, the gut absorbs only a fraction of dietary calcium. D3 (cholecalciferol) is made in skin from sunlight and comes from animal foods; D2 (ergocalciferol) is the plant and fungal form — both convert to the same active hormone in the liver and kidneys.

Sunlight, 10–20 min Salmon & mackerel Egg yolk Sun-dried mushrooms
Magnesium
The activator

Roughly 60% of the body's magnesium sits in bone. It's also required to convert vitamin D into its active form — low magnesium can leave vitamin D metabolically "stuck," even at good blood levels.

Pumpkin seeds Spinach Almonds Dark chocolate
Vitamin K2
The traffic controller

K2 activates a protein (osteocalcin) that binds calcium into the bone matrix — and another that keeps calcium out of artery walls. It's the least talked-about of the four, and arguably decides where all that calcium actually ends up.

Egg yolk Hard cheese Chicken liver
Why together

One chain, four links

Calcium is the brick. D3/D2 opens the gut door to let it in. Magnesium turns the key that activates vitamin D. K2 directs the calcium once it's in the bloodstream — into bone, not arteries. Loading up on calcium alone, without the other three, is the most common gap.

Chapter 02

Skin

Skin's firmness comes from a protein scaffold called collagen. The body can build its own — but only with the right cofactors on hand, and production slows year over year from your mid-twenties.

Collagen
The scaffold protein

Type I and III collagen make up most of skin's structural mesh. It thins with age and sun exposure; dietary collagen and its amino acids (glycine, proline) supply raw building blocks for the skin to keep rebuilding it.

Bone broth Fish skin Chicken skin
Vitamin C
The builder's cofactor

Collagen literally cannot be assembled without vitamin C — it's required by the enzymes that stabilize the collagen molecule. It's also a strong antioxidant that protects existing collagen from UV breakdown.

Amla & orange Bell pepper Guava & strawberry
Zinc
Repair & oil balance

Zinc supports wound healing and skin cell turnover, and helps regulate oil (sebum) production — one reason it's a go-to for acne-prone skin.

Pumpkin seeds Cashews Chickpeas
Omega-3 & Vitamin E
Barrier & glow

Omega-3 fats keep the skin's lipid barrier flexible and calm inflammation; vitamin E works alongside vitamin C as a fat-soluble antioxidant, protecting cell membranes from oxidative stress.

Walnuts & flaxseed Salmon Sunflower seeds
Why together

You can't out-supplement a vitamin C gap

Collagen supplements and bone broth supply the raw amino acids, but without enough vitamin C the enzymes that stitch them into a stable collagen fibre simply can't run at full speed. Pairing a vitamin-C food in the same meal — a squeeze of lemon, a side of amla — is what makes collagen intake count.

Chapter 03

Muscle, Tendon & Ligament

Muscle needs a steady protein supply and minerals for every contraction. Tendons and ligaments are quieter tissue — mostly collagen again — that heal slowly and reward the same nutrients that build skin, plus a few of their own.

Complete Protein
The rebuild material

Muscle fibres are in constant turnover — broken down and rebuilt daily. Protein spread across meals, with enough of the amino acid leucine, is what tips that balance toward growth and repair rather than net loss.

Eggs & paneer Lentils & dal Fish & chicken Greek yogurt
Potassium & Magnesium
Contraction & cramp control

Every muscle contraction and release runs on an electrical signal carried by potassium, magnesium, calcium and sodium moving across the cell membrane. Low potassium or magnesium is a classic, correctable cause of night cramps.

Banana Coconut water Sweet potato
Vitamin C, Manganese & Copper
Tendon & ligament repair

Tendons and ligaments are dense collagen with almost no blood supply, which is why they heal slowly. The same vitamin-C-dependent collagen synthesis applies here, plus manganese and copper — cofactors for the enzymes that cross-link collagen fibres into rope-like strength.

Citrus & amla Whole grains Cashews Shiitake
B6 & B12
Nerve-to-muscle signal

Muscles only move on instruction — B vitamins keep the nerves that fire them working properly, and support the energy metabolism that powers each contraction. B12 is worth watching closely on a vegetarian diet.

Eggs Dairy Fish
Why together

Muscle moves fast, tendon heals slow

Muscle rebuilds within days on enough protein and minerals — tendons and ligaments, being dense collagen with little blood flow, can take months. That's why an overuse injury there needs the same vitamin-C-and-collagen approach as skin, just with more patience.

Chapter 04

Roots & Herbs for Sleep

Alongside vitamins and minerals, Ayurvedic and herbal traditions lean on a handful of roots to quiet the nervous system before bed. Most are taken as a warm-milk decoction, tea, or standardized capsule roughly an hour before sleep.

Ashwagandha
Withania somnifera

An adaptogen — it helps the body regulate its stress response rather than sedating directly. Used traditionally to lower cortisol, ease anxiety, and make it easier to wind down at night.

Root · taken as churna or capsule
Tagara (Indian Valerian)
Valeriana wallichii / jatamansi

The Ayurvedic name for a native valerian species — a classic mild sedative root, used for restlessness and difficulty falling asleep, closely related to the valerian sold in the West.

Root · tea or capsule, near bedtime
Brahmi
Bacopa monnieri

A nervine tonic traditionally used to calm mental chatter and support memory. Often paired with ashwagandha in the evening for a quieter mind at lights-out.

Leaf · ghrita, syrup or capsule
Jatamansi
Nardostachys jatamansi

A Himalayan spikenard root, one of Ayurveda's oldest calming herbs — used for sleep, tension headaches, and to settle an overstimulated mind.

Root · warm milk decoction
Shankhpushpi
Convolvulus pluricaulis

A trailing herb used as a gentle nervine, traditionally taken to ease anxiety and support deeper, more settled sleep without next-day grogginess.

Whole herb · syrup or tea
Chamomile
Matricaria chamomilla

A gentle flower tea containing apigenin, which binds to calming receptors in the brain — the most familiar bedtime herb worldwide, easy to weave into a nightly ritual.

Flower · steeped as tea
